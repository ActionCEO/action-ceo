This was the first DePIN Day in Seattle, a full day of panels with the people building decentralized physical infrastructure, and I got to host it. DePIN means networks of real-world hardware (compute, wireless, GPS stations) run by lots of people instead of 1 company, and this stream is a good snapshot of where the builders thought it stood in October 2024.

## How the first DePIN Day came together

The final decision to do this event was made about a week and a half before it happened. A team worked behind the scenes to pull it off, with the Conduit Network and the Genzio team among the groups making it happen, inside a Seattle virtual production studio with a screen about 100 feet wide behind the stage. We streamed it on multiple YouTube channels and X accounts, and Binance streamed it too. [Watch at 15:32](?t=932)

I'll be honest: I had never been on a stage like that. Davey from Flux said on the first panel that "literally a week and a half ago" he got asked if he'd fly to Seattle. Everyone came out on short notice and agreed to talk about anything, which is exactly what you want from people who live this stuff every day.

## The problem with centralized infrastructure

The first panel was about how we got here. On stage: Brandon Baker from Conduit, Ken Anderson, CTO of Tashi, Davey, business officer at Flux, and Aaron, head of product at IoTeX. Aaron shared that the name "DePIN" itself came from a community vote about 2 years earlier, and that there are now thousands of companies in the sector. [Watch at 26:11](?t=1571)

I asked what's actually wrong with centralized infrastructure, because as a kid in the 90s I thought the internet was great. Davey's answer surprised the room: fundamentally, nothing is wrong with centralized services, except 1 big flaw. The single point of failure. If you have 1 provider, even with multiple data centers, it's still 1 provider. If you have thousands, and 1 goes down, there are 999 left. He'd just lived it: a patch from 1 company grounded his Delta flight and, as he put it, the world kind of stopped for half a day. [Watch at 32:58](?t=1978)

Ken's angle was different. Tashi built a fast, cheap consensus engine and tested it in gaming, and his team's rule is simple: add no friction. Take Web3 tech into the Web2 world and add value without making anything harder. That theme came up all day. Ken pointed out that a business has to predict its budget every month, so it can't have volatile coins sitting behind its tech. Davey's answer was that Flux takes PayPal and Stripe alongside its own token, with a discount if you pay in Flux, and handles the exchange part in the background. Most customers just want a 1-click solution, and the moment it feels like Web2 is the moment you get adoption.

## Where DePIN actually stood

After a break, the second panel moved from problems to what's working. David Chen, co-founder of GEODNET, joined Brandon, Ken and Davey. GEODNET runs a network of base stations that improve GPS accuracy by about 100 times. David said they'd reached almost 10,000 base stations, which he described as twice the size of the biggest Web2 competitor. Centimeter accuracy matters for things like robot lawnmowers, tractors and drones. [Watch at 2:16:37](?t=8197)

Davey kept it real: are we there yet? No. We're still very early, and most people don't realize how early. Another panelist made a point I keep repeating: DePIN might be the strongest bridge from Web2 to Web3, because it can show real customers, real utility and real revenue to people who think crypto has no business model. The catch is education. Most people don't understand the roads they drive on, and now we're asking them to own and run that infrastructure.

Then came my favorite part, on product market fit. People talking about your product on Twitter and Telegram is not the same as people paying you for it. The panel's take was that real projects start with customers who benefit, while some teams call themselves DePIN just to run a node sale or token sale and figure out the plan later. [Watch at 2:33:27](?t=9207)

## The future: the edge, privacy and on-prem

The third panel after lunch was the fun one, because we got to dream a little. Ken shared his: he runs his whole home on a private network, and he wants to plug in a Conduit device so he can tunnel back to it securely while traveling. He also wants to take the resources sitting idle at home, like a gaming PC and MacBooks, and make them available outside his home without exposing what's inside. For him it's about pushing everything to the edge and preserving privacy. [Watch at 4:38:11](?t=16691)

Brandon brought a number that stuck with me. He cited a report from a couple of months earlier saying 83% of enterprise CTOs and CISOs are putting strategic plans in place to move back on-prem, off the public cloud. His read: the market is raising its hand and saying it has a problem. If DePIN can offer a full decentralized stack without friction, he believes the opportunity is far bigger than today's crypto market. And if companies are already going back on-prem, the pitch is to turn that cost center into a profit center.

## What Conduit is building

To close, I asked Brandon to explain Conduit properly, because I still didn't fully get it. He described Conduit as a hardware-based layer zero, meaning security, identity and authorization start at the hardware level. It runs as a mesh network out to the edge, with small, widely dispersed data centers for redundancy. [Watch at 6:11:36](?t=22296)

On the economics, Brandon said there would be no token sale at all. Every token would be mined through what he called proof of economic activity, rewarding people for bringing real transactions to the network. At the time he said Conduit was launching in Q1 2025 and was in about 35 conversations with businesses. He also said they wanted to run DePIN Day every quarter, and we did come back for round 2, which you can read about in my [DePIN Day Seattle 2025 recap](/blog/depin-day-seattle-2025).
