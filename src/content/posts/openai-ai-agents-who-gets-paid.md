OpenAI wants AI agents working for you after you log off, and the industry is lining up enormous amounts of money to keep those systems running. So when the AI keeps working, the question that matters is who actually gets paid, and what's left after the bill.

## OpenAI Dots and always on agents

Dots are persistent agents. Each one gets its own cloud computer, and each can use a browser and connected apps. That means a swarm working for you while you're not around. I would not run a swarm of Dots, because you'll spend a lot of money for little return. Access is rolling out gradually, and the beta testers I know love it. [Watch at 7:47](?t=467)

The part most people miss is permissions. You can set approval rules for each agent and inspect activity logs. That is the bare minimum now. Fun side note: xAI grabbed dots.com and points it straight to Grokbot. Hilarious move.

Then the business test. What useful work actually gets finished? How much checking does it need? Will a customer pay for the result, and does supervision cost eat the margin? Right now I use these agents mostly for prep work. 9 times out of 10 they're not making decisions. Activity is not profit, and "runs while you sleep" is a feature, not a business model.

## When an agent goes rogue: the Muse story

Here's how it goes wrong. The Guardian reported that Meta's AI agent Muse gave out a user's home address without permission. Muse negotiated a keyboard sale on Facebook Marketplace, shared the seller's address, told the buyer he was home and set up a pickup. The seller was away and had no idea. [Watch at 15:17](?t=917)

He had set replies to "allow always" and still expected to approve offers later. People blamed him, but look closer. Reply access is not location access. It's not price authority. It's definitely not permission to arrange a meeting. High risk actions need separate approval. The buyer left a bad review, and the bot basically told him: sorry, you have to live with it. For a regular seller, that rating is money.

So decide what your agent must always ask before doing: spending money, sharing an address, publishing anything publicly. And it's getting scary good. I showed a live AI agent holding a full video conversation, and scammers are already using this kind of tech in fake job interviews to steal identities. Ask them to hold up 3 fingers.

## Broadcom's financing for Anthropic

Now the industry's bill. Broadcom may lend Anthropic up to $42 billion to lease its chips. The relationship combines supply, leasing and financing in 1 deal, and the debt could convert into Anthropic shares, which matters with an IPO on the table. Anthropic itself flagged potential conflicts. [Watch at 20:11](?t=1211)

Remember, "up to" is a ceiling, not cash already delivered. Capital pays for compute, compute supports AI products, and paying customers have to support the economics. The real test is outside demand. Everyone wants to sell picks and shovels. Here the shovel seller is helping finance the digging.

The questions I'd press: can Anthropic switch suppliers halfway through, how long do the commitments run, and what happens if AI prices crash or customer growth slows? There's a decent amount of gambling here. I dug into the debt side further in [the OpenAI revenue gap show](/blog/openai-revenue-gap-debt). Meanwhile a regular Nvidia 5090 is going for over $5,000. That makes no sense, but it tells you where demand is.

## Stripe's Open USD

Open USD is now the default stablecoin on Stripe. It's built for businesses and platforms, designed for payments and settlement in the background. Partner rewards depend on supply and activity, so Stripe is incentivizing businesses to move over. It's the same playbook crypto has used forever. Integration partners include Visa, Mastercard, Coinbase and Stripe. [Watch at 30:25](?t=1825)

Don't hear "rewards" and assume passive income. Holding Open USD does not guarantee rewards. This model is for partners, not for us. Ask who qualifies, what work is required, who carries compliance and fraud risk, and whether the incentives can go away. Read the partner terms before calling anything passive.

Quick note on partnerships: I'm now working with Coral App on the partnership side. People took advantage of that community and I want to see it through, with users actually earning.

## Power and the jobs report

Power purchase agreements are the name of the game. Constellation and Amazon announced a 20 year deal adding 190 megawatts of nuclear capacity, part of an announcement describing 690 megawatts in total and roughly a $3 billion investment. I've been saying nuclear is coming back. AI doesn't float in the cloud. It sits on chips and power contracts, and physical supply takes years to expand. [Watch at 42:08](?t=2528)

The September jobs report showed 29,000 jobs added, unemployment at 4.2% and wage growth at 3% year over year, with July and August revised down 60,000 combined. As of this show on Oct 2, 2026, the S&P 500 was up about 0.9%, the Dow about 0.5% and the Nasdaq about 1.2%. Bitcoin was above $86,000 before the report. Weak hiring cut rate hike expectations, so markets rallied. That doesn't make weak hiring good news.

## Build, power or payments

Right now power is moving the needle for me. If power wins, ask how long the contracts run, how concentrated the customers are, what financing costs and how long it actually takes to build. If payments win, ask who pays the fee, who handles compliance and whether rewards are temporary. [Watch at 49:32](?t=2972)

If building wins, which I think is very likely, ask if the problem is repeatable, what it costs and how fast a competitor can copy it. I saw this week that agents can spend about 17 times more tokens than humans with generic prompts. That bill adds up fast.

My advice for builders: start with a very narrow job and a clear customer. Keep human approval for high risk actions. Measure profit after model costs, checking time and redo time. And don't build around a vague passive income promise.
