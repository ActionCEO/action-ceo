DePIN Day came back to Seattle for round 2, and it was a much fuller house. I hosted a full day of panels with builders on telecom, decentralized AI, tokenization, IoT data and edge compute, which together give a clear picture of what DePIN projects were focused on at the start of 2025.

## Round 2, bigger than the first

We were back at the same Seattle studio with the giant screen, and from the stage it was obvious this one was bigger. During the day, a speaker from the Genzio team said this second DePIN Day came less than 6 months after the first, and that both the in-person and online crowds had about doubled. Jonathan from the studio also gave a shout out to about 55,000 people watching online. [Watch at 7:11](?t=431)

My job, besides moderating, was running the stream: reading your questions, pulling names off the Luma list and spinning the wheel for giveaways. We gave away DePIN devices and more. If you know how I feel about DePIN devices, you know why. Plug it in and let it rip. A big thank you to the sponsors who made the day possible, including IoTeX and Rogue.

## Telecom: why the big carriers need help

The first session was about telecom. On stage and on screen: Maria, the CEO of a telecom startup building distributed orchestration software, calling in from London with a PhD in wireless communications, Tyler, who runs product at a startup securing communication infrastructure, Ken Anderson, CTO of Tashi, who also comes from telecom, and Sunny from Conduit. [Watch at 14:52](?t=892)

Maria laid out the economics. According to her, 5G required almost 70% of revenues to be reinvested in new infrastructure, and nobody's bill went up enough to make that case. So operators need to cut costs somewhere, and sharing infrastructure is the natural way. DePIN, for her team, became a way to offer neutral infrastructure that telcos can access as a service. [Watch at 28:28](?t=1708)

Then the spectrum talk got spicy. A panelist explained that the FCC authorized 6 GHz for unlicensed use, which opens a lot of room for mesh and community networks that don't need to buy licensed frequencies at auction for billions. Another made a point about who pays the most for connectivity: as much as 40% can go to telecom services for the poor, they said. Ken brought it back to basics: tokens are there to keep devices up and running, but the real question is whether a network stays valuable longer than a fun 3 to 6 months. Start with the problem, then talk about the token.

## Decentralized AI: who actually builds it

Session 2 was AI. I had Davey from Flux, Matt, who does technology at the Solana Foundation, and Jesse Adams, co-founder and CEO of a mobile DePIN project on Solana with about 2.6 million installs, among others. The panel opened with the hard question: why do we even need decentralized AI when there are plenty of data centers, and $500 billion being thrown around? [Watch at 1:15:23](?t=4523)

Davey pushed back first: that money is from the companies themselves, not the government. His bigger point is that DePIN doesn't need those millions to get rolling. With Flux, anyone can contribute their own GPUs. Matt added that AI touches the whole stack, from data capture and annotation to monetizing open-source models, and that blockchains are really good at price discovery and value transfer for all of it.

We also talked about Jensen Huang's CES keynote, where he described the move from agentic AI into physical AI, like smart cities and self-driving vehicles. 1 panelist's answer: DePIN is going to power that next industrial revolution. Another pointed out why DePIN is such a good onboarding story. You already have an expensive phone you don't use all the time, and sensors keep getting cheaper. The idea that it works for you while you sleep is something everyone gets. If you want my more recent take on AI and crypto, my [Action Items show on the AI crash, Bitcoin and DePIN](/blog/action-items-ai-crash-bitcoin-depin) picks up that thread. [Watch at 1:49:33](?t=6573)

## Tokenization: proving the claim

After lunch we got into tokenization with Michael Hiles, founder and CEO of 10xTS, who's been in real world asset tokenization since about 2016 or 2017 and is also a founding partner at Conduit, plus Brandon Baker, a co-founder of Conduit. [Watch at 2:37:35](?t=9455)

I asked for the elevator pitch, and the first answer, from Michael, was the clearest one I've heard. A token is something that represents something else, like a digital twin. Minting it is easy. You can point at it in a wallet and say it equals a piece of an apartment complex or a designer handbag. But that's a claim, and the claim must be proven, immutably and in a way people can trust. [Watch at 2:40:51](?t=9651)

Another simple way the panel framed it: pennies aren't serialized, but every dollar bill has a unique serial number. That's the difference between fungible and non-fungible, and the exciting part is that both are now programmable. Proving the claim is still exactly what I look for in real asset projects today, like [Tezos and tokenized copper](/blog/tezos-copper-rwa-quantum-security).

## IoT, robots and who owns your data

The IoT session brought Aaron, head of product at IoTeX, who shared that IoTeX was working on a new white paper with a pivot toward AI and DePIN, and Mike Horton, project creator of GEODNET, which he described as the largest hyper precise location network in the world, positioning drones, farm tractors, survey equipment and robots with a GPS augmentation called RTK. [Watch at 3:24:47](?t=12287)

Elizabeth made a point every builder should tattoo somewhere: you have to grow supply and demand at the same time. Millions of Airbnb hosts in a city with no tourists is not a working system. Aaron showed a demand-side idea: an AI agent that pulls real-time DePIN data, like asking it for the current weather in Seattle from a decentralized weather network.

The part that hit home for me was data. 1 panelist said they don't want their daughter's data, from childhood all the way into adulthood, sitting with 1 cloud provider forever. Without a data bill of rights, they said, we have to take care of our own data, and that pushes you straight into decentralization. [Watch at 3:52:00](?t=13920)

## Edge compute and working together

Every session kept coming back to the edge, so we gave it its own panel. Ken explained that Tashi's consensus algorithm lets every player's machine host the game server and sync with the other players, cutting the dependence on expensive cloud servers for multiplayer games. That's a big deal for indie studios. [Watch at 4:30:36](?t=16236)

Ken also shared that IoTeX, Flux, Conduit, Tashi and GEODNET are part of a group called the Council of Decentralized Economies, which is working on standards. The goal is for projects to grow the whole DePIN economy, not just their own token, for example earning rewards on 1 network that you redeem for AI services on another. He also shared a line he credited to someone else that sums up DePIN: miners eat first. [Watch at 4:49:13](?t=17353)

## Real revenue is the new bar

Later in the day, a guest who researches how to evaluate DePIN networks described a valuation framework built around the risk profile between supply, demand and total market size. Their message matched what I heard on every panel: the first part of the pie is figured out. Now it's about demand and revenue, and that's how projects will be judged from here. It's no longer just a token play. [Watch at 5:44:00](?t=20640)
